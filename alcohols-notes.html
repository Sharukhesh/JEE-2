SECTION 1: Classification, Structure & Nomenclature

1.1 Detailed Classification

  • Monohydric, Dihydric, Trihydric:
    • Monohydric: 1 –OH group (e.g., CH3CH2OH).
    • Dihydric (Glycols): 2 –OH groups on different carbons (e.g., Ethane-1,2-diol / Ethylene Glycol: HO–CH2–CH2–OH).
    • Trihydric: 3 –OH groups (e.g., Propane-1,2,3-triol / Glycerol: HOCH2–CH(OH)–CH2OH).
  • Classification by Hybridization of C attached to –OH:
    • sp3 C–OH:
      • Alkyl Alcohols (1°, 2°, 3°): Depend on whether the α-carbon (bearing –OH) is attached to 1, 2, or 3 carbon atoms.
      • Allylic Alcohols: –OH is attached to an sp3 carbon immediately adjacent to a C=C double bond (e.g., CH2=CH–CH2OH, Prop-2-en-1-ol).
      • Benzylic Alcohols: –OH is attached to an sp3 carbon directly bonded to an aromatic ring (e.g., C6H5–CH2OH, Phenylmethanol).
    • sp2 C–OH:
      • Vinylic Alcohols (Enols): –OH is directly bonded to a carbon-carbon double bond (CH2=CH–OH). Unstable; rapidly tautomerizes to aldehydes/ketones (CH3–CHO).
      • Phenols: –OH is attached directly to an sp2 hybridized carbon of a benzene ring.
  • Ethers (R–O–R'):
    • Simple / Symmetrical: Both alkyl or aryl groups attached to oxygen are identical (e.g., C2H5–O–C2H5).
    • Mixed / Unsymmetrical: The two groups differ (e.g., CH3–O–C2H5 or C6H5–O–CH3).
JEE TrapGeminal Diols (1,1-Diols) & Rare Stable Hydrates Two –OH groups on the same carbon [R–CH(OH)2] experience intense electronic repulsion and spontaneously eliminate H2O to form C=O.
3 Famous Exceptions (Stable Gem-Diols asked in JEE):
  1. Chloral Hydrate [CCl3–CH(OH)2]: Strong –I effect of three –Cl atoms destabilizes the positive carbonyl carbon, and intramolecular H-bonding between –Cl and –OH locks the diol.
  2. Ninhydrin: Central carbon is flanked by two electron-withdrawing C=O groups, making the unhydrated tri-ketone electronically unstable due to severe dipole-dipole repulsion.
  3. Cyclopropanone Hydrate: Converts sp2 carbon (ideal 120° forced into 60°) to sp3 carbon (ideal 109.5°), relieving massive Baeyer angle strain.

2.3 All 4 Methods of Preparation of Phenols (Board & JEE Essentials)

  1. From Haloarenes (Dow's Process):
    Chlorobenzene is fused with NaOH at drastic conditions (623 K and 300 atm) to yield Sodium Phenoxide, which on acidification gives Phenol. (Mechanism at high temp proceeds via a Benzyne intermediate).
    C6H5Cl + 2NaOH &xrightarrow{623 K, 300 atm} C6H5O−Na+ + NaCl + H2O &xrightarrow{HCl} C6H5OH
  2. From Benzene Sulphonic Acid:
    Benzene is sulphonated with Oleum (H2S2O7), fused with molten NaOH (573–623 K), and acidified.
    C6H6 &xrightarrow{Oleum} C6H5SO3H &xrightarrow{1. NaOH (fuse), 2. H+} C6H5OH
  3. From Diazonium Salts (Laboratory Method):
    Aniline is diazotized with NaNO2 + HCl at 0–5°C (273–278 K) to form Benzene Diazonium Chloride, which upon warming with water releases N2 gas and forms Phenol.
    C6H5NH2 &xrightarrow{NaNO2 + HCl, 273-278 K} C6H5N2+Cl− &xrightarrow{H2O, Warm} C6H5OH + N2↑ + HCl
  4. From Cumene (Commercial / Industrial Method – HIGHEST WEIGHTAGE):
    Cumene (Isopropylbenzene, prepared via Friedel-Crafts alkylation of benzene with propene in H3PO4) undergoes aerial oxidation in the presence of O2 (free-radical mechanism at the benzylic 3° C–H bond) to form Cumene hydroperoxide. Treating it with dilute H2SO4 causes a 1,2-phenyl migration to yield Phenol and Acetone (valuable industrial co-product!).
    C6H5–CH(CH3)2 (Cumene) + O2 &xrightarrow{Air, ~400 K} C6H5–C(CH3)2–O–O–H &xrightarrow{Dil. H2SO4, 360 K} C6H5OH (Phenol) + CH3COCH3 (Acetone)

SECTION 3: Physical Properties & Acidity Trends

3.1 Boiling Points & Water Solubility

  • Intermolecular H-Bonding: Alcohols and phenols have much higher boiling points than ethers, haloalkanes, and hydrocarbons of comparable molecular masses due to strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
    Boiling Point Order (Comparable Mass): Carboxylic Acids > Alcohols > Aldehydes/Ketones > Ethers > Alkanes
    Example: Ethanol (MW = 46, BP = 351 K) >> Methoxymethane (MW = 46, BP = 248 K) > Propane (MW = 44, BP = 231 K).
  • Effect of Branching in Isomeric Alcohols: As branching increases, surface area decreases (molecule becomes spherical), weakening Van der Waals dispersion forces.
    Boiling Point of Isomeric Alcohols: 1° (Butan-1-ol) > 2° (Butan-2-ol) > 3° (2-Methylpropan-2-ol)
  • Solubility in Water: Lower alcohols (C1–C3) are miscible with water in all proportions due to H-bonding with water. As the hydrophobic alkyl chain (–R) grows larger, solubility decreases. Among isomers, branching increases solubility (3° > 2° > 1°) because a spherical alkyl group has a smaller hydrophobic surface area.
Board Classic Questiono-Nitrophenol vs. p-Nitrophenol (Steam Distillation & Boiling Point)
  • o-Nitrophenol forms intramolecular H-bonding (chelation within the same molecule, forming a 6-membered ring). Thus, attraction between separate molecules is weak → Lower Boiling Point, Steam Volatile, Less soluble in water.
  • p-Nitrophenol forms intermolecular H-bonding (associates multiple molecules together) → Higher Boiling Point, Not steam volatile, More soluble in water.
  • Conclusion: A mixture of o- and p-nitrophenol is easily separated by steam distillation (o-isomer distills over with steam!).

3.2 Acidity Masterclass (Guaranteed JEE & Board Topic)

A. Acidity of Alcohols (1° vs 2° vs 3°) & Comparison with Water

  • Alkyl groups are electron-releasing (+I effect). They increase electron density on the oxygen atom, making the O–H bond less polar and destabilizing the conjugate base (alkoxide ion, R–O−) by intensifying the negative charge and sterically hindering solvation by water.
  • Acidity Order in Aqueous Solution:
    CH3OH (Methanol, pKa = 15.5) > H2O (Water, pKa = 15.7) > 1° Alcohol > 2° Alcohol > 3° Alcohol (pKa ≈ 18)
    Note: Since water is a stronger acid than alcohols (except methanol), Alkoxide ions (R–O−) are stronger bases than OH−: R–O− + H2O → R–OH + OH−. Therefore, alcohols do NOT react with NaOH!

B. Why is Phenol (~1 Million Times) More Acidic than Ethanol? (Board 2-Mark Answer)

  1. Hybridization of Carbon: In Phenol, the –OH group is attached to an sp2 hybridized carbon (more electronegative), which pulls electron density away from oxygen, increasing O–H bond polarity. In ethanol, it is attached to an sp3 carbon.
  2. Resonance Stabilization of Conjugate Base:
    • Ionization of ethanol gives the ethoxide ion (C2H5O−), where the negative charge is strictly localized on the oxygen atom and further destabilized by the +I effect of –C2H5.
    • Ionization of phenol gives the phenoxide ion (C6H5O−), where the negative charge is delocalized over the ortho and para carbons of the benzene ring via resonance.
  3. Why Phenoxide ion is more stable than Phenol itself: Resonance structures of neutral phenol involve charge separation (+ve charge on O and −ve charge on the ring), which requires energy. Resonance structures of the phenoxide ion carry no charge separation (dispersal of negative charge only), making the phenoxide ion vastly more stable and driving the equilibrium toward H+ release!

C. Effect of Substituents on Acidity of Phenol (JEE Advanced Level)

  • Electron-Withdrawing Groups (EWG: –NO2, –CN, –CHO, –COOH, –X): Disperse the negative charge of the phenoxide ion → Increase Acidity (Lower pKa). Effect is much stronger at ortho and para positions than meta because resonance (–M / –R) operates only at ortho and para, whereas meta only experiences inductive (–I) effect.
  • Electron-Donating Groups (EDG: –OCH3, –NH2, –CH3): Intensify the negative charge → Decrease Acidity (Higher pKa).
Exact JEE Acidity Orders to Memorize
  1. Nitrophenols: p-Nitrophenol (pKa 7.1) > o-Nitrophenol (pKa 7.2) > m-Nitrophenol (pKa 8.3) > Phenol (pKa 10.0)
    Why is para slightly more acidic than ortho? In o-nitrophenol, intramolecular H-bonding locks the proton slightly, making H+ release marginally harder than in the para isomer!
  2. Number of –NO2 groups: 2,4,6-Trinitrophenol (Picric Acid, pKa 0.71) > 2,4-Dinitrophenol (pKa 4.0) > p-Nitrophenol (pKa 7.1) > Phenol (10.0)
  3. Cresols (Methylphenols): Phenol (10.0) > m-Cresol (10.1) > p-Cresol (10.2) > o-Cresol (10.3)
    Why? –CH3 shows +H (hyperconjugation) and +I at ortho/para, but only weak +I at meta. Since +I is distance-dependent, ortho has the strongest destabilizing +I effect.
  4. Methoxyphenols (–OCH3 has +M > –I, except at meta where only –I acts!):
    m-Methoxyphenol (–I only, more acidic than phenol!) > Phenol > o-Methoxyphenol > p-Methoxyphenol (+M dominates)
  5. Overall Organic Acidity Hierarchy (For NaHCO3 vs NaOH vs Na Tests):
    Sulphonic Acids (RSO3H) > Picric Acid > Carboxylic Acids (RCOOH, pKa ~4.8) > H2CO3 (pKa 6.4) > Phenols (pKa 10) > H2O (15.7) > Alcohols (16–18) > Terminal Alkynes (25)

SECTION 4: Chemical Reactions of Alcohols & Phenols

4.1 Reactions Involving O–H Bond Cleavage (Alcohols & Phenols Act as Nucleophiles)

  1. Reaction with Active Metals (Na, K, Al): Both alcohols and phenols liberate H2 gas. Phenols ALSO react with aqueous NaOH to form Sodium Phenoxide, whereas alcohols do NOT react with NaOH. Neither Phenol nor Alcohol reacts with NaHCO3 (except Picric Acid!).
  2. Esterification (Fischer Esterification & Acylation):
    • With Carboxylic Acids: R–OH + R'–COOH &xrightleftharpoons{Conc. H2SO4} R'–COOR + H2O. (Reversible! Water is removed continuously to shift equilibrium right).
    • Reactivity Order of Alcohols/Acids in Esterification (Steric Control): CH3OH > 1° > 2° > 3° and HCOOH > CH3COOH > RCH2COOH.
    • Isotopic Labeling Proof (JEE Must-Know): In R'–COOH + R–18OH → R'–CO–18OR + H2O, the acyl C–OH bond of carboxylic acid breaks and the O–H bond of alcohol breaks!
    • With Acid Chlorides (R'COCl): Carried out in the presence of Pyridine (base) to neutralize HCl formed and shift equilibrium forward.
    • Synthesis of Aspirin (Acetylation of Salicylic Acid – Board Favorite):
      2-Hydroxybenzoic acid (Salicylic Acid) + (CH3CO)2O (Acetic anhydride) &xrightarrow{H+} 2-Acetoxybenzoic acid (Aspirin) + CH3COOH
      Note: Salicylic acid + CH3OH / H+ gives Methyl Salicylate (Oil of Wintergreen); Salicylic acid + Phenol / POCl3 gives Phenyl Salicylate (Salol).

4.2 Reactions Involving C–O Bond Cleavage (ONLY Alcohols – Phenols Do NOT Cleave C–O easily!)

Why don't phenols show C–O bond cleavage with HX/PX3? Because of resonance, the C–O bond in phenol has partial double-bond character and the carbon is sp2 hybridized, making nucleophilic substitution extremely difficult.

A. Reaction with Hydrogen Halides (R–OH + HX → R–X + H2O) & Lucas Test

  • Reactivity of HX: HI > HBr > HCl (depends on bond dissociation enthalpy and nucleophilicity of X−).
  • Reactivity of Alcohols: Allylic / Benzylic / 3° > 2° > 1° (Proceeds via SN1 for 2° and 3° alcohols → Carbocation rearrangements occur! Proceeds via SN2 for 1° alcohols).
  • Lucas Reagent (Conc. HCl + Anhydrous ZnCl2): Anhydrous ZnCl2 acts as a Lewis acid that coordinates with the oxygen lone pair of –OH, converting poor leaving group (–OH) into a good leaving group (–O+H–ZnCl2−).
    • 3° (plus Allylic/Benzylic) Alcohols: White turbidity (insoluble alkyl chloride R–Cl) appears immediately.
    • 2° Alcohols: White turbidity appears in 5 minutes.
    • 1° Alcohols: No turbidity at room temperature (turbidity appears only on heating).

C. Acid-Catalyzed Dehydration of Alcohols to Alkenes

  • Ease of Dehydration (follows carbocation stability): 3° Alcohol > 2° Alcohol > 1° Alcohol
    • 1° Alcohol (Ethanol): Conc. H2SO4 at 443 K (170°C).
    • 2° Alcohol: 85% H3PO4 at 440 K.
    • 3° Alcohol: Mild 20% H3PO4 at 358 K.
  • Zaitsev (Saytzeff) Rule: The more substituted (more stable, having more α-hydrogens) alkene is the major product, and the trans-isomer is major over the cis-isomer. Always check for 1,2-hydride shift, 1,2-methyl shift, or ring expansion (4→5 or 5→6 membered ring) before eliminating H+!
Mandatory NCERT Mechanism #2Acidic Dehydration of Ethanol to Ethene (at 443 K) Equation: CH3–CH2–OH &xrightarrow{Conc. H2SO4, 443 K} CH2=CH2 + H2O
  • Step 1: Formation of protonated alcohol (Fast & Reversible):
    CH3–CH2–OH + H+ ⇌ CH3–CH2–O+H2 (Ethyl oxonium ion)
  • Step 2: Formation of carbocation by loss of water (Slowest / Rate-Determining Step):
    CH3–CH2–O+H2 → CH3–CH2+ + H2O
  • Step 3: Formation of ethene by elimination of a proton (Fast):
    H–CH2–CH2+ → CH2=CH2 + H+

4.3 Reactions of Phenols (Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution & Named Reactions)

The –OH group in phenol is a powerful activating and ortho/para-directing group (+M >> –I). In alkaline medium, phenol forms the phenoxide ion (C6H5O−), which is an even stronger ring activator than neutral –OH.

1. Halogenation (Solvent Effect – Board & JEE Favourite)

  • With Bromine in Low-Polarity Solvent (Br2 in CS2 or CHCl3 at 273 K): Phenol does not ionize into phenoxide ion; ring is only moderately activated → Gives Monobromophenols: p-Bromophenol (Major, ~80–90%) + o-Bromophenol (Minor). No Lewis acid (FeBr3) is required!
  • With Bromine Water (Br2 / H2O): Polar water facilitates ionization of phenol into the hyper-reactive phenoxide ion and polarizes Br2 heavily → Trisubstitution occurs immediately to yield a white precipitate of 2,4,6-Tribromophenol.

2. Nitration

  • With Dilute HNO3 at 298 K (25°C): Gives a mixture of o-Nitrophenol and p-Nitrophenol (separated by steam distillation).
  • With Concentrated HNO3 ( + Conc. H2SO4): Gives 2,4,6-Trinitrophenol (Picric Acid) (bright yellow solid).
    Board Note: Yield of Picric acid by direct nitration is very poor because conc. HNO3 is a strong oxidizing agent and oxidizes most of the phenol ring into tarry oxidation products. Modern Industrial Method: Phenol is first treated with conc. H2SO4 to form phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid (deactivates ring against oxidation), and then treated with conc. HNO3 to get a high yield of Picric Acid!

3. Kolbe's Reaction (Kolbe-Schmitt Reaction)

Phenol &xrightarrow{1. NaOH} Sodium Phenoxide &xrightarrow{2. CO2 (400 K, 4-7 atm), 3. H+} 2-Hydroxybenzoic acid (Salicylic Acid - Major)
  • Electrophile: Carbon dioxide (CO2), a weak electrophile. That is why phenol must first be converted into the much more nucleophilic phenoxide ion using NaOH!
  • JEE Advanced Twist (NaOH vs KOH): Using NaOH gives the ortho-isomer (Salicylic acid) as major because the small Na+ ion chelates both the phenoxide oxygen and CO2 oxygen in a 6-membered cyclic transition state. If KOH is used (larger K+ ion cannot chelate effectively), the para-isomer (4-Hydroxybenzoic acid) becomes the major product!

4. Reimer-Tiemann Reaction

Phenol &xrightarrow{1. CHCl3 + aq. NaOH (340 K)} [Intermediate: Benzal chloride salt] &xrightarrow{2. NaOH, 3. Dil. HCl} 2-Hydroxybenzaldehyde (Salicylaldehyde - Major)
  • Electrophile (JEE Frequent Question): Dichlorocarbene (:CCl2) — neutral, electron-deficient (6 valence electrons on carbon, singlet carbene, sp2 hybridized) formed via α-elimination from CHCl3 + OH−:
    CHCl3 + OH− ⇌ :C−Cl3 + H2O → :CCl2 (Dichlorocarbene) + Cl−
  • Variation: If CCl4 (Carbon tetrachloride) is used instead of CHCl3 with aq. NaOH, the final product after acidification is Salicylic Acid (2-Hydroxybenzoic acid)!

5. Reaction with Zinc Dust & Oxidation

  • Reduction with Zn Dust: C6H5OH + Zn (dust) &xrightarrow{Heat} C6H6 (Benzene) + ZnO.
  • Oxidation of Phenol: C6H5OH &xrightarrow{Na2Cr2O7 / Conc. H2SO4 (or Air)} p-Benzoquinone (a conjugated diketone). (In the presence of air, phenol slowly oxidizes to a dark pink/reddish-brown mixture containing quinones, known as phenoquinone).

SECTION 5: Ethers – Preparation & Chemical Cleavage

5.1 Methods of Preparation of Ethers

A. By Dehydration of Alcohols (Temperature-Dependent Reaction)

  • At 443 K (170°C) with conc. H2SO4: Ethanol undergoes intramolecular dehydration (elimination) to form Ethene (CH2=CH2).
  • At 413 K (140°C) with conc. H2SO4 (and excess alcohol): Ethanol undergoes intermolecular nucleophilic substitution (SN2) to form Ethoxyethane (C2H5–O–C2H5).
Mandatory NCERT Mechanism #3Acidic Dehydration of Ethanol to Ethoxyethane (at 413 K) Equation: 2 CH3CH2OH &xrightarrow{Conc. H2SO4, 413 K} CH3CH2–O–CH2CH3 + H2O (Bimolecular SN2 Mechanism)
  • Step 1: Protonation of one ethanol molecule:
    CH3CH2–O–H + H+ ⇌ CH3CH2–O+H2
  • Step 2: Nucleophilic attack (SN2) by a second unprotonated alcohol molecule on the protonated alcohol:
    CH3CH2–OH + CH3CH2–O+H2 → CH3CH2–O+(H)–CH2CH3 + H2O
  • Step 3: Deprotonation to yield symmetrical ether:
    CH3CH2–O+(H)–CH2CH3 → CH3CH2–O–CH2CH3 + H+
Limitations of this method (Board 2-Mark Q):
  1. Suitable only for preparing symmetrical ethers from 1° alcohols.
  2. For 2° and 3° alcohols, steric hindrance prevents SN2 attack, and elimination (E1) dominates to give alkenes as the main product!

B. Williamson Ether Synthesis (Best Method for Both Symmetrical & Unsymmetrical Ethers)

R–O−Na+ (Sodium Alkoxide/Phenoxide) + R'–X (Alkyl Halide) &xrightarrow{SN2} R–O–R' + NaX
Golden Rules of Williamson Synthesis (100% Exam Frequency) Since Williamson synthesis proceeds strictly via an SN2 mechanism, the alkyl halide (R'–X) MUST be unhindered (Methyl or 1°)! Because alkoxides (R–O−) are not only strong nucleophiles but also powerful bases, using a 2° or 3° alkyl halide triggers E2 Elimination instead of substitution!

5.3 Electrophilic Substitution in Aromatic Ethers (Anisole)

The alkoxy group (–OCH3) is ortho, para-directing and strongly activating (+M > –I). Due to steric hindrance of –OCH3, the para-isomer is always the major product (~90%).

  • Bromination: Anisole + Br2 in Ethanoic acid (CH3COOH) → p-Bromoanisole (Major, 90%) + o-Bromoanisole (Minor, 10%). (No FeBr3 catalyst required!).
  • Friedel-Crafts Alkylation: Anisole + CH3Cl &xrightarrow{Anhyd. AlCl3, CS2} 4-Methoxytoluene (Major) + 2-Methoxytoluene (Minor).
  • Friedel-Crafts Acylation: Anisole + CH3COCl &xrightarrow{Anhyd. AlCl3} 4-Methoxyacetophenone (Major) + 2-Methoxyacetophenone (Minor).
  • Nitration: Anisole + Conc. HNO3 + Conc. H2SO4 → 4-Nitroanisole (Major) + 2-Nitroanisole (Minor).

SECTION 6: The "JEE Advanced" Extension Block

6.1 Pinacol–Pinacolone Rearrangement (1,2-Diols + Acid)

When a vicinal diol (1,2-diol) is heated with dilute or conc. H2SO4, it dehydrates and rearranges into a carbonyl compound (ketone/aldehyde).

(CH3)2C(OH)–C(OH)(CH3)2 (Pinacol) &xrightarrow{H2SO4, Heat} CH3–CO–C(CH3)3 (Pinacolone, 3,3-Dimethylbutan-2-one) + H2O
  • Rule 1 (Which –OH leaves first?): Always protonate and remove the –OH group that produces the most stable carbocation (e.g., Benzylic / 3° > 2° > 1°; or ring bearing electron-donating –OCH3 over –NO2).
  • Rule 2 (Which group migrates from the adjacent carbon? – Migratory Aptitude Order):
    The group with the highest electron density migrates to the carbocation to give a resonance-stabilized protonated carbonyl (oxocarbenium ion):
    Migratory Aptitude: –H (Hydride) > –Aryl with EDG (p-CH3O–C6H4 > p-CH3–C6H4 > –C6H5 > p-Cl–C6H4) > 3° Alkyl > 2° Alkyl > 1° Alkyl > –CH3
  • Rule 3 (Ring Expansion in Spiro/Cyclic Diols): If one –OH is on a cyclopentane ring and the adjacent –OH is on an acyclic carbon [e.g., 1-(1-hydroxy-1-methylethyl)cyclopentanol], loss of the acyclic –OH causes the 5-membered ring C–C bond to migrate, expanding it into a stable 6-membered cyclohexanone ring!

6.2 Oxidative Cleavage of Vicinal Diols by Periodic Acid (HIO4) or Lead Tetraacetate [Pb(OAc)4]

  • Condition: Must be a 1,2-diol (vicinal diol), α-hydroxy aldehyde/ketone, or 1,2-diketone (capable of forming a cyclic periodate ester; therefore, trans-1,2-diols in rigid bicyclic/diaxial systems do NOT cleave!).
  • Shortcut Trick to Find Products & Moles of HIO4 Consumed:
    1. Every C–C bond between two adjacent carbons bearing –OH (or =O) consumes 1 mole of HIO4.
    2. Break that C–C bond and attach one –OH to each broken end. Dehydrate any resulting gem-diols!
    • –CH2(OH) (Primary alcohol end) &xrightarrow{HIO4} HCHO (Formaldehyde)
    • –CH(OH)–R (Secondary alcohol end) &xrightarrow{HIO4} R–CHO (Aldehyde)
    • –C(OH)R2 (Tertiary alcohol end) &xrightarrow{HIO4} R2C=O (Ketone)
    • –CH(OH)– (Middle carbon flanked by two –CH(OH)– groups, e.g., Glycerol / Glucose) &xrightarrow{HIO4} HCOOH (Formic Acid)
    Example: Glycerol [CH2(OH)–CH(OH)–CH2(OH)] + 2 HIO4 → 2 HCHO + 1 HCOOH + 2 HIO3 + H2O.

6.3 Ring-Opening of Epoxides (Oxiranes) – Regioselectivity & Stereochemistry

Both acidic and basic ring openings of epoxides proceed with 100% Anti-attack (Inversion of configuration at the attacked carbon), but the site of nucleophilic attack flips completely:

Condition Reagents Which Carbon Does Nucleophile Attack? Reason
Basic / Neutral Medium (Pure SN2) R–O− / ROH, OH−, RMgX, LiAlH4, CN− LESS Substituted (Less Sterically Hindered) Carbon Pure steric control (standard SN2 backside attack).

SECTION 7: Commercially Important Alcohols (NCERT Board Section)

7.1 Methanol (CH3OH – "Wood Spirit")

  • Why called Wood Spirit? Originally produced by the destructive distillation of wood.
  • Modern Industrial Preparation (Catalytic Hydrogenation of Water Gas / Syngas):
    CO + 2H2 &xrightarrow{ZnO–Cr2O3, 200–300 atm, 573–673 K} CH3OH
  • Toxicity (Board 1-Mark Q): Drinking even small quantities of methanol causes blindness, and larger quantities cause death. Inside the liver, methanol is oxidized to methanal (formaldehyde), which rapidly reacts with cellular proteins and causes the protoplasm to coagulate (just like an egg coagulates on cooking).

7.2 Ethanol (C2H5OH – "Grain Alcohol")

  • Commercial Preparation by Fermentation of Molasses / Cane Sugar:
    1. C12H22O11 (Sucrose) + H2O &xrightarrow{Invertase (from Yeast)} C6H12O6 (Glucose) + C6H12O6 (Fructose)
    2. C6H12O6 (Glucose / Fructose) &xrightarrow{Zymase (from Yeast)} 2 C2H5OH (Ethanol) + 2 CO2↑
    • Conditions for Fermentation: Temperature ~298–303 K, anaerobic conditions (absence of air/O2). If air gets in, ethanol is oxidized to ethanoic acid (vinegar) by Acetobacter bacteria, ruining the taste of alcoholic drinks!
    • Concentration Limit: Zymase enzyme gets inhibited once ethanol concentration exceeds ~14%.
  • Important Industrial Terms:
    • Rectified Spirit: 95.5% Ethanol + 4.5% Water (constant-boiling azeotropic mixture).
    • Absolute Alcohol: 100% Pure Ethanol.
    • Power Alcohol: 20% Ethanol + 80% Petrol (with benzene as co-solvent) used as motor fuel.
    • Denaturation of Alcohol (Board Favorite): Commercial ethanol is made unfit for human consumption by adding Copper Sulphate (CuSO4) (to give it a blue color) and Pyridine (a foul-smelling liquid) or methanol. This is called denatured alcohol (methylated spirit).

8.2 How to Distinguish Common Pairs in 1 Sentence (Board 2-Mark Ready Answers)

  • Propan-1-ol vs. Propan-2-ol:
    • By Iodoform Test: Propan-2-ol has a CH3CH(OH)– group and gives a yellow precipitate of CHI3 on warming with I2/NaOH; Propan-1-ol does not.
    • By Lucas Test: Propan-2-ol (2°) gives turbidity within 5 minutes with conc. HCl + anhyd. ZnCl2; Propan-1-ol (1°) gives no turbidity at room temperature.
  • Methanol vs. Ethanol: Ethanol gives a yellow precipitate of CHI3 in the Iodoform test (I2 + NaOH); Methanol does not.
  • Phenol vs. Ethanol (or Cyclohexanol / Benzyl alcohol): Phenol gives a violet coloration with neutral FeCl3 (and a white ppt with Bromine water); Ethanol/Cyclohexanol/Benzyl alcohol does not.
  • Phenol vs. Benzoic Acid: Benzoic acid gives brisk effervescence of CO2 with aq. NaHCO3; Phenol does not react with NaHCO3.
